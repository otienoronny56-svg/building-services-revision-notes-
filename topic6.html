Day 1: Basics & Regulatory Framework

1. General Introduction

High-rise buildings face huge challenges in moving people and goods. Mechanical transportation (Lifts, Travelators, Escalators, Hoists) improves traffic flow, reduces congestion, increases property value, and boosts rental demand.

2. Regulatory Framework in Kenya

Governed by the Kenya Building Code, NCA guidelines, KEBS, and County Governments.

  • Key Requirement 1: Buildings with more than 4 floors MUST have lifts.
  • Key Requirement 2: Buildings above 18m height require firefighting lifts.

3. Primary Functional Requirements

  • Safety: Paramount. Lifts must not be used in a fire (except specific firefighting lifts).
  • Reliability & Maintenance: A lengthy shutdown is expensive and disruptive.
  • Quiet and smooth operation: Traction lifts offer a gentler, faster ride than hydraulic lifts.
  • Speed: Hydraulic lifts max out around 1 m/s. Traction lifts are typically 1.6 m/s but can reach 6 m/s.
  • Coordination and Tolerances: The lift shaft must be plumb. Critical dimensions include pit depth and headroom.

Day 2: Lifts (Classifications & Types)

1. Means of Raising/Lowering

  • Traction Elevators: Car and counterweight attached to opposite ends of hoist ropes passing over a driving machine. Run on load-bearing rails. Used in mid/high-rise buildings (5+ floors).
  • Hydraulic Elevators: Raised by forcing pressurized oil through a valve into a steel cylinder. The piston bears the weight (no load-bearing rails needed). Used in low-rise (2-5 floors). Uses power only on ascent; gravity descends. Oil can be recycled.

2. Classification Based on Use

  • Platform lifts: Low-rise, move slowly, often for disabled access.
  • Passenger lifts: Carry people, strict safety standards.
  • Service lifts: Carry goods, may not meet passenger safety standards.
  • Dumb waiters: Small lifts used in kitchens/restaurants for food.
  • Trolley lifts: Transport large goods on roll cages.
  • Stair lifts: Motorised seat on stairs (not for wheelchairs).
  • Evacuation lift: Used as part of a management plan during a fire.
  • Firefighting lift: Additional protection and direct control for fire services. Required if floor is >18m above or >10m below access level.

Day 3: Lift Planning & Control

1. General Planning

Lifts should be located centrally to minimize horizontal travel. In tall buildings (>15 storeys), high-speed express lifts bypass lower floors.

2. Lift Control Systems

  • Single Automatic: Called by one person/group. "In use" light stays on until they leave. Good for nursing homes/small flats.
  • Down Collective: Stores calls. As it descends, it answers in floor sequence. Most suited for flats/small hotels (traffic between lobby and specific floors).
  • Full/Directional Collective: Two directional buttons. Responds to calls in floor order first in one direction, then the other. Best for offices and department stores.
  • Two or More Cars: Central processor coordinates. One car waits at lobby, the other at mid-point.

3. Lift Safety Features

  • Buffers: Located at the shaft base. Oil loaded for >1.5m/s speeds, otherwise spring loaded.
  • Overspeed governor: Pulley system that responds to centrifugal force. Over-speeding switches off power and actuates the safety braking gear.
  • Safety gear: Hardened steel wedges that slow/stop the car by frictional contact with the guide rail.

Day 4: Escalators

1. Escalator Basics

Moving stairs arranged in pairs. Max capacity depends on step width (600, 800, 1000mm) and speed (0.5 to 0.65 m/s). They can transport up to 12,000 persons/hour. The motor runs continuously.

Components include: Landing platforms, Truss, Tracks (Step wheel track & trailer-wheel track), Steps, and Handrail.

2. Types of Escalators

  • Parallel: Side-by-side. Transports more people/unit time than a straight escalator and carries more weight.
  • Stairway: Built into a staircase (inclined moving stairs). Good for limited space.
  • Belt-Type: Most common. Rectangular steps with slightly curved outer edges.
  • Curved: Eye-catching, efficient use of space (radius allows longer arc). Expensive and requires more maintenance.
  • Step Type: Stepped conveyors. Usually descending, strictly controlled direction.

3. Pros & Cons

Pros: High capacity, continuous movement, space-saving vs traditional stairs.

Cons: High initial/maintenance costs, energy consumption, safety risks (slips/entrapment), and limited capacity during extreme peak periods.

Day 5: Travelators

1. What is a Travelator?

Also known as autowalks, passenger conveyors, or moving pavements. They provide horizontal conveyance for distances up to 300m. Slight inclines up to 12° are common (18° max, but not for wheeled transport).

2. Types

  • Horizontal: Airports, malls.
  • Inclined: Moving between elevation changes.
  • Dual-Directional: Two conveyors moving in opposite directions.
  • Multi-Speed & Circular: Variable speeds or spiral loops.
  • Outdoor: Weatherproofed for rain/snow.
  • Speed walks: Constant speed, faster than walking.

3. Features & Challenges

Features: Moving surface (metal slats or rubber), Handrails, Safety Sensors, Emergency Stop, Anti-Slip surface.

Disadvantages: Massive space requirements, high energy consumption, reliability issues (breakdowns with heavy use), and limited functionality (they only move horizontally or slightly inclined).

Day 6: Hoists & Installation Process

1. Hoists

Mechanical devices for lifting heavy loads vertically. Parts include: Power Source (motor/hydraulic), Drum/Pulley (wraps the chain), Lifting mechanism (chain/wire rope), and Control System.

Merits: Efficiency, safety, versatility. Demerits: High initial cost, space requirements, complexity.

2. 8-Step Installation Process for Lifts/Escalators

  1. Site Survey: Measure shaft space, check power, study traffic.
  2. Shaft Construction: Must have correct pit depth and overhead space. Done during construction.
  3. Electrical Planning: 3-phase power, backup generators, surge protection.
  4. Delivery: Motors, rails, cabins arrive.
  5. Mechanical Installation: Rails fixed, motor installed, cabin fitted, doors installed.
  6. Electrical Install: Control boards programmed, buttons tested (Lift becomes "smart").
  7. Testing: Tested under full load, fire mode, and brake failure.
  8. Handover: Certified for use, maintenance contract begins.

Stop usage if: Jerky movement, strange noise, burning smell, or system errors occur.
