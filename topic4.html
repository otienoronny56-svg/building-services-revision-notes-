Day 1: Solid Waste Classifications

1. What is Solid Waste?

Waste (rubbish, trash, refuse, garbage, junk, litter) is unwanted or useless materials. Solid waste disposal refers to all techniques used for the collection, treatment, and disposal of a community's solid waste. The Sustainable Waste Management Act (2022) provides the legal framework, emphasizing the 5Rs (Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Repair, Refurbish).

2. Source-Based Classification

  • Residential: Dwellings, apartments (leftover food, vegetable peels, plastics, clothes).
  • Commercial: Stores, restaurants, markets, hotels, auto-repair shops, medical facilities.
  • Institutional: Schools, colleges, offices, prisons (mainly paper, plastic, glasses).
  • Municipal: Dust, leafy matter, building debris, street cleaning, landscaping.
  • Industrial: Process wastes, ashes, demolition, hazardous wastes.
  • Agricultural: Spoiled food grains, vegetables, agricultural remains from fields/farms.
  • Open areas: Streets, alleys, parks, vacant lots, beaches.

3. Type-Based Classification

  • Garbage: Putrescible (rotting) animal and vegetable wastes from food. Obnoxious odor, attracts rats.
  • Ashes and residues: Remaining from burning wood/coal. Mostly inorganic, valuable in landfills.
  • Combustible vs. Non-combustible: Combustible = paper, cardboard, textile, rubber. Non-combustible = glass, crockery, tin, aluminum, dirt.
  • Bulky wastes: Refrigerators, washing machines, furniture, tires, trees. Require special collection.
  • Street wastes: Paper, dirt, leaves collected from walkways and alleys.
  • Biodegradable vs. Non-biodegradable: Degraded by micro-organisms vs inorganic/recyclable materials.
  • Construction and demolition wastes: Earth, stones, concrete, bricks, lumber, plumbing.
  • Hazardous wastes: Dangerous due to ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, toxicity (e.g., solvents, paints, pesticides, pathological wastes from hospitals).
  • Sewage wastes: Solid by-products of sewage treatment (organic sludge).

Day 2: Solid Waste Management (SWM) System

1. What is an SWM System?

A combination of functional elements facilitating the collection and disposal of solid wastes at minimal costs, preserving public health, and ensuring minimal environmental impact.

2. The Functional Elements of SWM

  1. Waste generation: Wastes are generated at the start of any process and at every stage as raw materials become goods. The key here is identification of waste.
  2. Waste storage: Collection never takes place precisely at generation time. Heterogeneous wastes in residential areas must be removed within 8 days due to storage shortage and biodegradable rotting. Options: plastic bins, dustbins, used oil drums, sacks, skips. Problem in Kenya: irregular collection leads to overflow.
  3. Waste collection: Gathering wastes and hauling them to a transfer station, processing plant, or disposal site. Depends on containers, frequency, and management arrangements.
  4. Transfer and transport: Transferring wastes from smaller collection vehicles to larger ones. Subsequent transport over long distances to disposal sites (e.g., trucks transporting to Dandora Dumpsite).
  5. Processing: Altering physical and chemical characteristics for energy recovery and recycling. Includes compaction, thermal volume reduction, incineration, composting.
  6. Recovery and recycling: Separation of valuable resources, size reduction, density separation (air classifiers, magnets for iron, screens for glass).
  7. Waste disposal: The ultimate fate of solid wastes (landfills). Land use planning is the primary determinant. Modern sanitary landfills confine wastes to the smallest area and cover them daily.

Day 3: Refuse Disposal & Incinerators

1. Refuse Disposal Systems

Methods include: Open burning, Dumping into the sea, Sanitary Landfills, Incineration, Composting, Ploughing in fields, Grinding into sewers, Salvaging, Garchey system.

2. Incinerators & Their Parts

Incineration is the combustion of organic substances in waste, converting it into ash, flue gas, and heat. It requires negative pressure to prevent smoke misdirection.

  • Primary Chamber: Where waste is initially loaded and burned at high temperatures.
  • Secondary Chamber: Breaks down remaining pollutants and gases from primary combustion.
  • Flue Gas Stack (Chimney): Releases emissions safely into the atmosphere.
  • Control Systems: Monitor and regulate the process.
  • Air Pollution Control Devices: Scrubbers, filters, electrostatic precipitators remove pollutants.
  • Ash Handling System: Collects and disposes of ash residue.
  • Heat Recovery System: Captures heat to produce steam or heat buildings.

Principle: A primary burner receives diesel oil to ignite, then receives sludge oil. Atomizing air facilitates combustion. Gases burn out in the secondary chamber.

3. Types of Incinerators

  • Rotary Kiln: Slanted primary chamber that rotates. Waste is kept circulating continually. Good for pharmaceutical, medical, and hazardous wastes.
  • Fluidized Bed: Air is injected through nozzles into a bed of granular material (limestone/silica sand) keeping it violently agitated (fluid-like). Thermal efficiency up to 90%. Good for liquid waste/sewage sludge.
  • Moving Grate: Waste is burned in layers on a moving grate. Ash falls into a water trap as slag (10-25% of weight). Expensive but handles large units.
  • Waste-Gas Flare: Burns excess gases in oil/gas extraction or landfills. Produces greenhouse gases, heat, and noise.
  • Multiple Hearth: Row of circular hearths. Material moved by rotating rabble arms. 1400-1800°F.
  • Catalytic: Uses a catalyst to accelerate oxidation and limit NOx emissions at low temps.
  • Liquid Injection: Uses high pressure to split liquid wastes into tiny droplets for rapid burning (1200-3000°F).
  • Fixed Grate / Direct-Flame: Used when waste gas contains particles. Runs between 1000 and 1500°F.

Day 4: Pros & Cons of Incinerators

1. Applications of Incinerators

  • Medical waste: From labs/hospitals. Destroy and neutralize health risks.
  • Animal waste: Carcasses and by-products to prevent spread of infection/viruses.
  • General waste: From mining/military operations.
  • Nanomaterial wastes: From consumer products and nanotechnology research.

2. Merits of Incinerators

  • Reduces the total amount of garbage by up to 95% and the solid portion by 80-85%.
  • Disposes of difficult-to-recycle waste (unlike landfills which only break down organics).
  • Waste-to-Energy: Converts waste into heat or power (electricity).
  • Requires less space than landfills and can be located closer to cities, reducing transport drives.
  • Generates less unpleasant odor than landfills (no open-air rotting).
  • Does not emit methane (unlike landfills).
  • Extremely high temperatures kill hazardous bacteria, pathogens, and toxins.
  • Not restricted by variations in the weather.

3. Demerits of Incinerators

  • Can produce air pollution: nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur oxides, and particulate matter.
  • Produces bottom ash and fly ash, which may contain harmful metals.
  • Not a sustainable solution (does not conserve resources).
  • Construction requires expensive infrastructure and equipment. Expensive to operate.
  • Long-term negative health effects on surrounding communities (cancer, birth defects) due to low exposures to metals/pollutants.
  • Requires highly experienced and expensive labor to administer.

Day 5: Refuse Chutes

1. What is a Refuse Chute?

An inclined channel or vertical pipe system passing from floor to floor, allowing refuse to be dropped from each floor down to a central refuse room on the ground floor/basement. The most common type is the dry-garbage chute. The chute shaft should be 400mm in diameter, made from asbestos/cement pipes, and strictly vertical.

2. Parts of a Refuse Chute

  • Chutes: The vertical pipe. Should be smooth internal finish, stainless steel or concrete.
  • Vent: Installed at roof level to remove foul smell and gases.
  • Inlet Hopper: The receptacle fitting/hatch on each floor for receiving refuse.
  • Collection Chamber: Compartment at the lower end. Must have drainage via a 100mm trap to the sewer, shutters to prevent rats, and 1-hour fire resistance.

The collection chamber should be at least 2.5m high, 1.5m wide, 2.5m long, and accessible by a refuse vehicle (within 25m). The chute should be sited away from habitable rooms, but max 30m horizontal distance from any dwelling.

3. Types of Chutes

  • Vertical: Straight down. Most common in high-rises.
  • Angled / Curved: When vertical installation isn't feasible due to space/obstacles.
  • Recycling: Segregates different types of recyclable materials.
  • Compactor: Integrated with compactors to compress waste, saving space.
  • Odour-Control: Equipped with seals, filters, deodorizers.
  • Fire-Rated: Built with fire-resistant doors/closures to stop flames spreading.

4. Advantages & Disadvantages

Pros: Convenience (no carrying bags down stairs), space efficiency, improved hygiene (enclosed), safety (avoids accidents carrying heavy bags on stairs), aesthetics.

Cons: Odour accumulation if poorly maintained, blockages, cross-contamination of recyclables, fire hazards from combustibles, and limited capacity during peak usage.

Day 6: Garchey System & Landfills

1. The Garchey System

Refuse (potato peelings, small bottles, ashes) is collected in a unit below the kitchen sink in each flat. A bowl retains normal waste water. Refuse is placed in a central tube. When raised, the waste water and refuse are carried down a stack to a chamber at the base of the building.

A specially equipped tanker collects it weekly, compacting it into a damp, semi-solid mass. One tanker serves 200 dwellings. Waste water from the tanker is discharged to a foul sewer.

Merits: High hygiene (sealed system controls vermin and odour), infrastructure doubles for wastewater, very convenient.

Demerits: The evacuation tank takes up a whole kitchen cabinet, prone to blockages/back-flow, hard to separate for recycling, expensive maintenance, high water usage, unsuitable for bulky/dry waste.

2. Sanitary Landfills

Waste is placed in a large excavation (pit/trench) and backfilled with excavated soil daily. Ideally, 0.5m of soil should cover the deposited refuse at the end of the day to prevent animals digging and flies breeding.

Key establishment factors:

  • Siting: The most difficult stage. Requires Environmental Impact Assessment and community involvement.
  • Design: Needs impermeable lining, gas monitoring, fencing, and post-closure maintenance.

3. Constructing Landfills

The basic element is the refuse cell (compacted in thin layers up to 3 meters deep). The compacted refuse occupies one-quarter of its loose volume. Several adjacent cells make up a lift. Lifts are stacked on top of each other. The final cap may have topsoil for vegetation. Crawler tractors spread and compact the soil.

4. Advantages & Disadvantages of Landfills

Advantages: Convenience, cost-effectiveness (cheaper than incineration), sanitation (centralized), Energy production (landfill gas can generate electricity), and Land reclamation (can become parks when capped).

Disadvantages: Environmental impact (leachate contaminates groundwater, methane gas), Limited lifespan (fills up, hard to find new sites), Not sustainable (buries recyclables), Health risks (pollution, vectors), Aesthetic concerns (ugly, displacement).
