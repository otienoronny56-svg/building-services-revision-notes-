Day 1: Terminology & Wastewater Types

1. What is Drainage?

Drainage is the system of pipes and channels that remove wastewater, sewage, and rainwater from a building and transport it to disposal or treatment systems. It operates primarily through gravity using angled pipes.

Common issues include: Slow/blocked drains (caused by grease, fat, roots), improper grading (slope directs water toward property), and overflowing gutters.

Key terminology:

  • Invert level: The depth to the bottom of a drain pipe measured from the datum.
  • Gully: A fitting on the drain that takes waste pipes from baths and basins but not Water Closets.
  • Public Sewer: The large pipe laid below public land carrying waste to treatment works.
  • Surface water drainage: Water from roof/paved areas after rain. Considered clean, doesn't need sewage treatment.

2. The Three Types of Wastewater

In building services, wastewater is divided into three main categories:

  1. Black Water (Soil Water): Contains human waste and pathogens (from WCs, urinals, bidets). Has high bacteria levels, organic matter, strong odors. Must be transported to municipal sewers, septic tanks, or biodigesters for proper treatment.
  2. Grey Water: Does not contain human waste, but contains soap, grease, and food particles (from sinks, showers, baths). Contains less harmful bacteria but is still not safe for drinking. It can be recycled for landscape irrigation and toilet flushing in sustainable buildings.
  3. Storm Water (Rainwater Runoff): Collects on roofs, balconies, driveways, and pavements. It is carried away by gutters, downpipes, surface drains, and catch basins.

3. Uses of Storm Water in Building Services

Storm water drainage prevents flooding, structural damage, soil erosion, and water stagnation. Once collected, storm water can be used for several non-potable purposes:

  • Landscape Irrigation: Soft water (low mineral content) is beneficial for plants.
  • Toilet Flushing: Significant portion of indoor water consumption. Saves water bills.
  • Cleaning and Washing: Cars, pavements, construction equipment.
  • Construction Activities: Concrete curing, dust suppression, soil compaction.
  • Groundwater Recharge: Directed to soak pits or infiltration trenches to replenish aquifers.
  • Cooling Systems: Used in cooling towers and HVAC systems.
  • Fire Fighting Storage: Stored in dedicated backup tanks for sprinklers and hose reels.

Day 2: Drainage Principles & Systems

1. Principles of Soil Drainage

Since drains laid below ground are fairly inaccessible, their operations must be trouble-free. The following principles must be followed:

  • Ensure drains are watertight so waste does not leak and contaminate the ground.
  • Clear away obstructions (like surplus mortar at joints).
  • Lay drains on even gradients to make sure water carries solid matter smoothly.
  • Lay drains in straight lines. If a drain changes direction, insert an inspection chamber.
  • Construct manholes where several pipes join a single drain run, or at 90-meter intervals on straight lines.
  • Construct a manhole at the boundary before the drain joins the public sewer.
  • Use soil drains at least 100mm in diameter.
  • Surround drains under buildings in at least 150mm of concrete.

2. The Combined System

Most common in historically established urban areas (Nairobi, Mombasa). A single pipe network carries both foul water (sewage) and surface water (stormwater).

  • Advantages: Cost-effective (single infrastructure), efficient in light rain.
  • Challenges: Pollution risks (untreated sewage discharged into rivers during heavy rains - "Combined Sewer Overflows"), overflow risks (surcharging/backups), and it places unnecessary burden on treatment plants to treat rainwater.

3. The Separate System

The modern standard. The National Building Code mandates that stormwater shall not enter a sewer installation. This system uses separate foul water drains (leading to a sanitary sewer) and surface water drains (leading to rivers/soakaways).

  • Found in: Newer residential estates (Syokimau, Ruiru).
  • Advantages: Reduced pollution of natural water bodies, and highly efficient focused treatment of wastewater.
  • Challenges: Infrastructure costs are higher (two separate systems).

4. Surface Drainage

Common in rural & peri-urban areas. It is the natural/artificial removal of excess water from the surface of the land. It relies on two physical principles:

  • Gradient (Slope): Land is graded so gravity pulls water away from buildings. A common residential grade is 2% (2 units drop for every 100 units distance).
  • Collection Points: Structures that "catch" the moving water.

Includes Open Channels (Invert Block Drains), Swales, Gutters, Catch Basins (Gully Pots), French Drains, and Channel (Trench) Drains.

Day 3: Ground Water & Subsoil Drainage

1. Subsoil Drainage

Unlike surface drainage (which deals with water before it enters the soil), sub-surface (subsoil) drainage deals with water after it has soaked into the ground. It permanently lowers the natural water table.

2. French Drains / Soak Pits

Common in private compounds with no municipal sewer. They allow water to seep naturally into the ground.

  • Structure: A trench filled with rounded gravel and a perforated pipe (PVC or HDPE) at the bottom.
  • The Filter Concept: In professional installations, the entire gravel bed is wrapped in a geotextile fabric. This allows water to pass but stops fine soil particles from clogging the pipe.
  • Application: Used around building perimeters ("footing drains") to protect basements/crawlspaces.

3. Mole Drains

A unique, "pipeless" form of sub-surface drainage used in heavy clay soils (like Black Cotton soil).

  • How they are made: A tractor pulls a "mole plow" (a vertical blade with a cylindrical bullet) about 400mm-600mm below the surface.
  • Mechanism: The bullet packs the clay soil tight, forming a continuous, unlined horizontal tunnel (temporary pipe).
  • Lifespan: They eventually collapse or silt up, lasting 2 to 5 years before needing to be "re-malled."

4. Interceptor Drains & Ground Water Pumps

Interceptor (Cut-off) Drains: Placed strategically to "intercept" groundwater moving laterally through soil, usually on a slope. If a house is on a slope, water hits the uphill foundation. The interceptor drain is placed uphill to catch the water and carry it around the building. They are deeper than standard drains.

Ground Water Pumps: Used to drain water from aquifers to maintain/lower the water table. Pumping creates a "drawdown" of groundwater away from the pump point.

Day 4: Access to the Drain

1. Why Do We Need Physical Access?

Drainage systems are "out of sight, out of mind." Physical access points are strictly required for three reasons:

  1. Inspection and Monitoring
  2. Maintenance and Clearing Blockages (Rodding)
  3. Where there is a change of Direction or Gradient

Access is provided at significant changes in direction, gradient, pipe size, junctions, at the head of a drain, and on long straight runs.

2. Types of Access Points

  • Shallow Access Chambers: Small compartments (up to 600mm deep) providing drain access in both directions and possibly into a branch. An inexpensive application for shallow depths. Sometimes combined with rodding points.
  • Inspection Chambers: Larger than access chambers, with an open channel and space for several branches. Typically up to 1 meter deep with a removable cover. Large enough to look inside or insert tools, but not large enough for a person to physically enter. Built from uPVC, concrete, or bricks.
  • Manholes: Large chambers with sufficient space for a person to gain access at the drain level. When invert depth exceeds 1m, step irons must be provided at 300mm vertical and horizontal spacing. A built-in ladder is used for very deep chambers.

3. Backdrop Manholes

Where there is a significant difference in level (a large drop) between a drain and a private/public sewer, a backdrop manhole is used to reduce excavation costs.

Instead of sloping the drain steeply, it is laid flat and the water "drops" vertically at the manhole. For cast iron/uPVC pipes up to 150mm, the vertical backdrop pipe may be secured inside the manhole. Otherwise, it is located outside the manhole and surrounded with concrete.

Day 5: Inspection & Testing

1. Preliminary & Pre-Commissioning Checks

Preliminary: Scrutinize the system design, layout, building codes, and material adherence.

Pre-Commissioning: Visual inspection of the physical condition, verifying materials match specifications, and closely inspecting access points (manholes, rodding eyes) for secure covers and effective sealing.

2. Leak Testing

  • Water Test: The system is plugged and filled with water to check for leaks. Requires a test bend and an extension pipe to produce a 1.5 m head of water. The system should stand for 2 hours. Maximum allowed leakage for 100mm/150mm pipes is 0.05 and 0.08 litres per metre run over the next 30 mins.
  • Air Pressure Test: Tests the system's structural integrity against designated pressures. Water seals are filled, and airbags/expandable bungs are inserted into the ends of stack pipes. Air is hand-pumped to 38 mm water gauge using a U-tube manometer. The pressure must remain constant for 3 minutes. Soap solution is used on joints to reveal leaks.
  • Smoke Test: Used for existing stacks. Smoke from a cartridge or oil generator is injected. It is less severe than the air test. Suitable warnings must be given to occupants!

3. Functional Tests & Commissioning

Flow Test: Controlled flow is implemented to observe proper flow rates, identify restrictions, and ensure the system conveys wastewater without backups.

Connections & Ventilation: Verify the integrity of joints. Inspect vent pipes and air admittance valves to prevent airlocks.

Commissioning: Comprehensive records are compiled. The final inspection verifies compliance, officially declaring the system ready for operation and handing it over to the end user.

Day 6: Waste Management Systems

1. Soak-Away

A covered, porous-walled chamber that allows water to slowly percolate into the ground. It is the secondary chamber receiving effluent from a septic tank. Can be traditional (filled with large stones/hardcore) or modern (geocellular plastic crates with 95% void space). Success depends on soil permeability (requires a percolation test) and location (at least 5m from a foundation, 3m from a septic tank, and downhill from drinking water).

2. Cesspool

A completely sealed underground holding tank. It performs NO treatment of the waste. Once full, it must be vacuumed out by an exhaust tanker ("honey sucker"). Extremely expensive to maintain due to frequent emptying. Used temporarily when other options aren't viable. Sited on sloping ground, >7m from building, <30m of vehicle access.

3. Septic Tanks

Watertight chambers that receive black and grey water. Solids settle out and break down, while liquid effluent overflows into a soak-away. They require emptying every few years. They need large volumes of flushing water and permeable soil for the soak-away. Rainwater should never enter a septic tank. Must be >15m from a water supply and >3m from a building.

4. Biodigester

A modern, enclosed, oxygen-free (anaerobic) system using specialized bacteria to actively "digest" sewage into treated water and biogas. Highly popular in Kenya (Syokimau, Ruiru) due to space efficiency (small plots) and resilience in high water tables.

  • Products: Treated water (for irrigation), Biogas (for cooking/lighting), and Bio-slurry (organic fertilizer).
  • Maintenance: Rarely needs exhausting (unlike septic tanks). Odorless. Fast to install (1-2 days).
  • Warnings: Harsh chemicals (bleach/acids) will kill the beneficial bacteria! Non-biodegradables (plastics) cause blockages.
