Day 1: Law & Fire Prevention Basics

1. Kenyan Fire Regulations

Fire prevention and control in Kenya are governed by the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSHA), 2007, and the Fire Risk Reduction Rules of 2007. Neglecting these rules can lead to fines up to KES 500,000.

Statutory Requirements:

  • Annual safety audits.
  • Fire drills must be conducted at least once every 12 months.
  • Clear emergency routes that lead directly to safety, with doors opening in the direction of escape. Sliding/revolving doors cannot be used as emergency exits.

2. Causes of Fire in Kenya

Fires in places like Gikomba Market, Industrial Area, and informal settlements (Kibera) are worsened by poor electrical wiring, overcrowding, lack of fire equipment, and limited access roads for fire engines.

3. Passive vs. Active Controls

  • Passive Controls: Built-in safety features that do not require activation. They use non-combustible materials to contain fire, delay structural collapse, and protect escape routes. Examples: Fire-resistant walls, Fire-rated doors, Compartmentation.
  • Active Controls: Equipment installed to fight the fire when it occurs. They respond to the fire. Examples: Fire extinguishers, Hose reels, Sprinklers, Fire alarms.

4. Emergency Protocols

  • RACE: Rescue, Alarm, Contain, Evacuate.
  • PASS: Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep (for using a fire extinguisher).

Day 2: Classes of Fire & Extinguishers

1. Classes of Fire

  • Class A (Ordinary Combustibles): Wood, paper, textiles, rubber (e.g., timber yard fires).
  • Class B (Flammable Liquids): Gasoline, kerosene, cooking oil, paints (e.g., petrol station).
  • Class C (Electrical Equipment): Faulty wiring, overloaded circuits, appliances.
  • Class D (Combustible Metals): Magnesium, titanium, sodium. Require specialized agents because they burn with intense heat.
  • Class K/F (Kitchen Fires): Cooking oils and fats in deep fryers.

2. Fire Extinguisher Color Codes

Fire extinguishers are handheld active devices for "first attack" firefighting. They must be serviced annually and have inspection tags. Note: Sand and water buckets are no longer acceptable as a first-aid fire facility.

  • Red (Water): For Class A fires. Cools the fire. Never use on electrical or oil fires!
  • Cream (Foam): For Class A and Class B. Formed by a chemical reaction between sodium bicarbonate and aluminum sulphate.
  • Blue (Dry Powder): "ABC" powder. Versatile for A, B, and C class fires.
  • Black (Carbon Dioxide): For Class B and C (electrical). Leaves no residue. Pressurized liquid converts to gas.
  • Yellow (Wet Chemical): Specially designed for Class K/F (cooking oils).
  • White (Water Mist): Suitable for most fire classes.

Day 3: Water-Based Fire Systems

1. Fire Hose Reels

Hand-operated device to guide a fire hose. Used as a first-aid measure by occupants. They should be located where users are safe (e.g., staircase landing).

  • Capacity: Must discharge 0.4 l/s at a 6m distance.
  • Pressure: 200 kPa required at the highest reel. (If main water cannot provide this, a suction tank & booster pump are needed).
  • Sizing: 50mm pipe for buildings up to 15m height. 65mm pipe for buildings taller than 15m. Hose length up to 45m covers 800 m².
  • Mandatory in: Shopping malls, Universities, Office buildings.

2. Fire Hydrants (Dry & Wet Risers)

A fitting in a street or public place where a fire hose is attached to a water main. Firefighters use them to supply water to the building's standpipes (risers). Water is pumped up the building through a riser into each floor (which has a hydrant landing valve).

  • Dry Riser: Only charged with water during an emergency.
  • Wet Riser: Water is always present in the pipe.

3. Drenchers

Unlike sprinklers (which are internal), drenchers are normally found on the exterior of a building to protect against a fire spreading from a neighboring building. All nozzles in a drencher system are activated simultaneously (sprinklers open individually).

  • Roof drenchers: On the roof ridge, throw a curtain of water upwards.
  • Wall/Curtain drenchers: Throw a curtain of water over openings.
  • Window drenchers: Positioned horizontally level with the top of a window.

Day 4: Sprinkler Systems

1. How Sprinklers Work

A sprinkler head is an automatic "open once only" tap. When heated, it opens at a pre-set temperature, spraying pressurized water to wet combustibles and cool the smoky layer. As pressure drops, it automatically activates an alarm to call the fire service.

2. Types of Sprinkler Systems

  • Wet system: Permanently charged with water. Used where temps are above freezing.
  • Dry system: Charged with compressed air. When a head breaks, air escapes and water rushes in. Not commonly used in Kenya (we don't have freezing weather).
  • Alternative wet and dry: Water in summer, air in winter.
  • Tail end system: A wet system where only specific unheated extensions (tail ends) are charged with air. Max 100 sprinklers on a tail end.
  • Pre-action system: Used in data centers/server rooms. Pipe is dry to avoid accidental water damage. If a head breaks, only an alarm sounds. Water only flows if a separate heat detector on the ceiling confirms a fire (set to a lower temp than the sprinkler).
  • Recycling pre-action: Turns off the water after a 5-min delay when the fire is subdued. If fire restarts, it re-engages.

3. Water Supplies for Sprinklers

  • Elevated private reservoir: 9m³ to 875m³.
  • Suction tank: Combined with booster pumps.
  • Gravity tank: Located on a tower to provide water pressure (head).
  • River or canal: Requires strainers and duplicate pumps (one diesel, one electric).

Day 5: Smoke Control & HVAC Dampers

1. Fire Dampers in Ventilation Ductwork

Fire and Smoke Dampers are passive fire protection products designed to close when temperature rises, preventing the spread of fire/smoke inside the HVAC ductwork.

How they operate:

  • Fusible Link: Melts at about 70°C to release a steel shutter.
  • Electromagnet: An electromagnet holds the shutter open. A fire/smoke detector sends a signal to deactivate the magnet, dropping the shutter. (This is better, as smoke damage occurs before heat reaches 70°C).
  • Intumescent-coated honeycomb damper: In the presence of heat, the coating expands to 100 times its original volume, sealing the duct. Used only in low-velocity systems.

Dampers are majorly found in hospitals, airports (JKIA), and high-end commercial buildings.

2. Pressurization of Escape Routes

In high-rise buildings (above 4-5 floors), stairways and lobbies may be air pressurized (injecting clean air) to clear smoke and provide a safe escape route. The stairwell has a higher pressure (25 to 50 Pa) than the surrounding accommodation area, pushing smoke away from the stairs.

Operating Methods:

  • Switched on automatically by a smoke detector.
  • Runs fully during all hours of occupancy.
  • Runs continuously at reduced capacity, but jumps to full output upon fire detection.

Day 6: Detectors, Alarms & The Kenyan Context

1. Types of Fire Detectors

  • Human sense: Manual activation (break-glass). Unreliable if the area is empty.
  • Thermal (Heat) detectors: Sense abnormal rise in temperature. Easy to install but may not detect fire in its early smoldering stages. Common in offices.
  • Smoke detectors: Detect fire at the smoldering stage (very early). More expensive but highly efficient. Common in kitchens.
  • Flame detectors: Very expensive and reliable in hostile environments. Must be directly looking at the flame. Used in industrial plants.

2. Alarm Output Devices

  • Bells: Most common.
  • Horns: Ideal where loud signals are needed (libraries, sensitive structures).
  • Chimes: Soft alarm tone (hospitals, theatres).
  • Speakers: Can reproduce recorded voice evacuation messages (large multi-storey buildings).
  • Visual alarms: Strobes used in high-noise areas or for hearing-impaired occupants.

Crucial: Because of frequent power outages in Kenya, buildings must have backup batteries/generators for alarms.

3. Challenges in Kenya

Common challenges include poor enforcement of regulations, lack of maintenance culture, corruption in approvals, and informal settlements lacking infrastructure.

  • Gikomba Market: Electrical faults + congestion. No hydrants, poor access.
  • Industrial Area: Flammable chemicals. Needs sprinklers and hazard classification.
  • School Dormitories: Arson/electrical faults. Needs fire drills and clear escape routes.
