Day 1: Terminology & Introduction

1. Introduction to Sanitary Systems

In buildings, many types of sanitary appliances are required. They fulfill a variety of specialized functions connected with the drainage and disposal of sewage and refuse from buildings. They can be divided broadly into waste appliances and soil appliances.

  • Soil water (Black water): Wastewater containing human waste from toilets, urinals, or bidets.
  • Wastewater (Grey water): Water from sinks, showers, baths, and washing machines. It contains dirt, grease, soap, or food particles, but no human waste.

2. Sanitary Appliances vs. Sanitary Fittings

  • Sanitary Appliances or Fixtures: These are sanitary objects or appliances that are fixed or attached. They are commonly referred to as sanitary furniture. They are static, immovable, and permanently connected to a plumbing system for the reception and discharge of water or waste. Examples: Water closet (WC), washbasin, urinal, floor drain, flushing cisterns, showers, faucets, bathtubs, sinks, bidet.
  • Sanitary Fittings: Also known as hygienic fittings or sanitary clamps. These are objects used to seal, clamp and fasten two or more separate ends of sanitary fixtures to maintain a high hygienic standard. They create closures, linkages, and secure connections so there are no leaking gaps for bacteria. Examples: Adapter, coupling, sleeve, union, cap, plug, elbow, tee, wye, cross, reducer.

3. Soil System & Sewerage System

  • Soil System: Refers to the network of pipes and fittings responsible for collecting and conveying wastewater from sanitary fixtures to the sewer or sewage treatment system.
  • Sewerage System: The entire network of pipes, pumps, and other infrastructure designed to collect and transport wastewater from various sources to treatment plants or disposal points.
  • Effluent: The treated or untreated liquid discharge from a wastewater treatment system, septic tank, or other plumbing appliance.

4. Traps & Water Seals

Trap: A device designed to retain a small amount of water to prevent the passage of sewer gases into living spaces while allowing wastewater to flow freely.

Water seal: A small amount of water retained in the U-shaped bend of a plumbing trap (like P-traps or S-traps) under fixtures. It acts as a hygienic, airtight barrier.

  • Purpose: To block methane, hydrogen sulfide, and other hazardous gases, odors, bacteria, and pests.
  • Mechanism: The U-bend retains water after a fixture is used, creating a physical barrier.
  • Potential Failures: The seal can be lost through evaporation, backpressure, or siphoning.
  • Solutions: Adding water, using a trap seal primer, or a specialized one-way valve trap seal.

Grease Trap: A device installed in commercial kitchens to capture and retain grease and solids before they enter the wastewater disposal system.

Backflow Prevention: The installation of devices or methods to prevent the reversal of flow in a drainage system which could contaminate the potable water supply.

Day 2: Sanitary Appliances

1. Washbasins (LAV-US) & Sinks

Washbasins: The standard basin consists of a bowl, weir overflow, and holes for taps. It is supported on brackets or a pedestal which conceals the pipework. For best functionality, it should:

  • Have a slotted overflow hole.
  • Have internal angles designed to facilitate cleaning.
  • Have a circular waste hole in the bottom.
  • Have an integral soap holder recess that drains into the bowl.
  • Have a tapering rubber plug fitted in the outlet to hold water.

Sinks: Rectangular shallow receptacles suitable for kitchens or laboratories. The floor of the sink slopes towards the circular waste hole. Kitchen sinks typically have a draining board on the right or both sides (double bowl double drain). Overflow slots are provided.

2. Bathtubs & Bidets

Bathtubs: Sinks come in stainless steel, porcelain, and cast iron. Bathtubs must be provided with one over-flow pipe to take excessive water. The waste pipe of the bath is provided with a trap to prevent foul gases from entering the bathroom.

Bidets: A sanitary appliance used for personal hygiene after using the toilet. It typically involves spraying water onto the genital and anal area.

3. Water Closets (W.C.)

A WC is a sanitary appliance to receive human excreta directly and is connected to the soil pipe by means of a trap. They are classified into:

  1. Squatting type or Indian type
  2. Wash-down, Pedestal or European type

Features of modern WCs include elongated oval/D-shaped bowls, adjustable nozzles (for combined bidets), smooth non-porous surfaces for efficient cleaning, and options for hygienic drying.

Flushing Cisterns: Used for flushing WCs and urinals. Provided with an inlet pipe, overflow pipe, and an automatic closing float ball valve.

4. Urinals & Faucets

Urinals: Used to collect urine. The inside surface is regular and smooth for efficient flushing. The bottom slopes towards the outlet for drainage.

  • They are washed at intervals of 20 minutes by an automatic flushing cistern discharging 4.5 liters of water per 610 mm of stall width.
  • Types: Slab type, Stall type, Squatting plate (mostly ladies' lavatories).

Faucets: Control the flow of water from pipes to sinks, bathtubs, showers, and bidets. Styles include single-handle, double-handle, touchless, and sensor-activated.

Day 3: Sanitary Fittings

1. Connection Types

  • Ball and sleeve fittings: Connects an outer sleeve to an inner (ball) fitting. The sleeve retracts to connect/disconnect.
  • Barbed fittings: Connects flexible hose tubing via a barbed end with a tapered stub and ridges. Best suited for low-pressure applications (not a strong seal).
  • Cam-lock fittings: Connected using tabs which fold down into the receiver to lock the fitting. Used in heavy-duty applications (fire hose, sludge pumping).
  • Compression fittings: Connect vessels using compression on a gasket, ring, or ferrule.
  • Crimp fittings: Placing a vessel over a tubular end and crimping against it with a sleeve/crimp socket using crimping tools.
  • Plain ends: Allow vessels to be connected by adhesive or solder.
  • Flanges: Used to connect pipes, pumps, valves. Fixed using welding/threading and sealed with bolts. Allows for easy cleaning and inspection.

2. Fittings that Extend or Terminate

  • Adapters: Connect unlike pipes (make ends male or female threaded). Used heavily for PVC and copper pipes.
  • Couplings: Used to stop leakages in broken or damaged pipes. Connects pipes of the SAME diameter permanently.
  • Unions: Similar to a coupling but can be removed easily at any time.
  • Caps & Plugs: Used to close ends of a pipe. Plugs are fitted inside the pipe and threaded. Caps go over the outside and can be glued/soldered.

3. Fittings that Add or Change Directions

  • Elbows: Change the direction of flow. Standard types are 90-degree and 45-degree angles.
  • Tees: T-shaped fitting with one inlet and two outlets at 90 degrees. Used to merge flow. If all sides are the same size, it's an equal tee; otherwise, unequal tee.
  • Cross: Contains four openings in four major directions. Used in fire sprinkler systems.
  • Wyes (Y-fittings): Used in drainage systems to keep water flow smooth with a branch at 45 degrees. Used when sanitary tees fail to work horizontally.

4. Reducers and Regulating Valves

Reducers: Change vessel size to reduce flow size. Two kinds: Concentric reducer (cone shape, gradual) and Eccentric reducer (one flat edge facing the mouth to reduce air accumulation).

Regulating Fittings: Gate valves, globe valves, angle valves, check valves (spring/swing), ball valves, metered valves, thermostatic valves, pressure reducing valves, sensor operated valves.

Day 4: Traps & Water Seals

1. Know Your Installations (Trap Geometries)

Traps are not separate systems—just different geometries. They provide a water seal.

  • P-Trap: Shape looks like the letter P. Used where the waste pipe goes through the wall. Most common in modern plumbing.
  • S-Trap: Shape looks like S. Used where the pipe goes through the floor. Can cause self-siphonage (water seal loss) more easily.
  • Q-Trap: Similar to P-trap but with a slightly different bend. Less common.

2. Specific Trap Types

  • Gully Trap: Built external to the building to carry wastewater from sinks/washbasins to the nearest sewer. Profound seal traps with a water seal depth of at least 50 mm. Prevents foul gases and cockroaches from entering house pipes. Available in P, S, and Q shapes.
  • Floor Trap: Receives wastewater from showers, bathroom floors, and kitchens, transmitting it to drainage pipes while preventing foul sewer gases.
  • Bottle Trap: Given to get squander from washbasins and kitchen sinks where the apparatus doesn't have an underlying trap. Shaped like a bottle.
  • Intercepting Trap: Installed in the Interceptor Manhole (Chamber) at the junction of the building sewer and the public sewer. Prevents foul gases from the public sewer from entering the building's drainage network.
  • Grease Trap: Introduced in waste lines from food handling units to isolate and hold grease. Can be cleaned from the surface.

3. Loss of Trap Water Seal

The most obvious cause of water seal loss is leakage due to defective fittings. Otherwise, it is caused by poor system design/installation:

  • Self-siphonage: As an appliance discharges, water fills the waste pipe and creates a vacuum that draws out the seal. Caused by waste pipes that are too long, too steep, or too small in diameter.
  • Induced siphonage: The discharge from one appliance draws out the seal of an adjacent appliance by creating a vacuum in a shared branch pipe. Common in undersized shared waste pipes or stacks.
  • Back pressure: Compression occurs due to resistance to flow at the base of a stack, displacing water in the lowest trap upwards. Caused by a bottom bend radius being too small or the lowest branch being too close to the stack base.
  • Capillary action: A piece of rag, string, or hair caught on the trap outlet slowly wicks water out over the edge.
  • Wavering out: Gusts of wind blowing over the top of the vent stack cause a partial vacuum, disturbing water seals.
  • Evaporation: During prolonged inactivity, water evaporates. Handled by manually adding water or using trap seal primers.

Day 5: Sanitary Pipe Works

1. The 5 Types of Drainage Water Pipes

There are 5 primary types of drainage water pipes used in building construction.

  1. Soil Pipes: Designed to carry soiled (black) water from the toilet or urinal to the sewer. Soil pipes must be vented high at the top of a building (soil pipe stacks) to allow harmful and foul gases produced by waste to vent safely into the atmosphere.
  2. Waste Pipes: Often a smaller diameter pipe that carries grey wastewater from sinks, washing machines, showers, and baths. It does not carry human waste, so it doesn't need the exact same venting system as a soil pipe (it generates fewer harmful gases), but it still requires proper drainage slopes.
  3. Vent Pipes: Attached to the top of soil and waste pipes specifically for the release of bad odors and to prevent siphonage of water seals by balancing the air pressure in the drainage system.
  4. Rainwater Pipes: Attached to the roof or open areas to collect rainwater. Drawn to the ground level to be removed or connected to a rainwater harvesting tank. Generally made of PVC.
  5. Anti-Siphonage Pipes: Connected to the outlets of toilets and fixtures. Their specific job is to maintain the water seal by preventing induced or self-siphonage, ensuring foul gases cannot enter bathrooms. Made of PVC and must conform to exact sizing specifications.

Day 6: Plumbing Systems

1. Introduction to Plumbing Drainage Systems

Plumbing is the network of pipes, tanks, and fittings used inside a building for supplying water and removing water-borne wastes. Drainage plumbing systems follow the general laws of gravity and pressure. There are four principal systems adopted in building drainage.

2. Two Pipe System

This is considered the best and most improved type of plumbing drainage system.

  • Two sets of vertical pipes are laid: one strictly for draining soil (black water) and another for draining sullage (grey waste water).
  • Soil fixtures (latrines, urinals) connect via branches to the vertical soil pipe.
  • Waste fixtures (baths, sinks, wash-basins) connect via branches to the vertical waste pipe.
  • Crucially, the soil pipe and the waste pipe are separately ventilated by providing their own separate vent pipes.

3. One Pipe System

  • Instead of using two separate pipes, only one main vertical pipe is provided.
  • This single vertical pipe collects both the soil water and the sullage water from their respective branch pipes.
  • The main pipe is ventilated at its top with a cowl.
  • In addition to the main pipe, a separate vent pipe is provided to ventilate all the traps, preventing siphonage.

4. Single Stack System

  • A single pipe system without providing any separate ventilation pipe.
  • It uses only one vertical pipe to carry both sewage and sullage.
  • There is no separate vent pipe network for the traps.
  • Instead, the single main stack is extended up to about 2 meters higher than the roof level and fitted with a cowl to allow foul gases to escape. This requires very strict design of branch lengths and slopes to prevent self-siphonage.

5. Partially Ventilated Single Stack System

  • An improved form of the single stack system.
  • In this system, only the traps of the water closets (soil appliances) are separately ventilated by a relief vent pipe.
  • The waste appliances (sinks, baths) rely on the main stack for ventilation.
  • It uses two pipes (like the one-pipe system), but the cost of branch ventilation is considerably reduced since waste traps don't get individual vent pipes.
