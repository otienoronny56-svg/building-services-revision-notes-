Day 1: Indoor Air Quality & Pollutants

1. The Irony of Airtight Buildings

As buildings become more airtight to save energy, an unintended consequence is the accumulation of internally generated contaminants. This leads to deficient Indoor Air Quality (IAQ).

2. The EPA Statistics

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA):

  • The average person receives 72% of their chemical exposure at home.
  • Indoor pollutants may be 2 to 5 times (sometimes 100x) higher than outdoor levels.
  • People spend about 90% of their time indoors.

3. Common Pollutants in Kenya

  • Smoke from cooking fuels (charcoal, kerosene, LPG).
  • Dust and vehicle exhaust (urban areas).
  • VOCs (Volatile Organic Compounds) from paints, furniture, and cleaning detergents.
  • Carbon monoxide (faulty appliances).
  • Mold and dampness due to high humidity.

Day 2: Reasons to Ventilate

Why Implement Ventilation?

  1. Maintaining IAQ: Dilutes VOCs, CO, and particulate matter.
  2. Regulating Temperature: Exchanges indoor and outdoor air to maintain a comfortable thermal level.
  3. Controlling Humidity: Removes humid air produced by cooking and showering, preventing mold, dampness, and condensation.
  4. Removing Pathogens: Crucial in crowded spaces to minimize airborne transmission of viruses and bacteria.
  5. Enhancing Comfort: Reduces stuffiness and unpleasant odors (odor removal).
  6. Safety: Dilutes combustible gases and carbon monoxide.
  7. Compliance: Meeting building codes and regulations for health and safety.

Day 3: Design Considerations

1. Building Specific Factors

  • Location: External air quality dictates if natural ventilation is possible. Poor outside air requires mechanical filtration.
  • Geometry: Volume, shape, and organization affect airflow. We use CFD (Computational Fluid Dynamics) software to model this.
  • Construction: Older buildings have high infiltration (leaks). Modern buildings are airtight, requiring higher forced ventilation rates.
  • Materials & Finishes: Paints and carpets emit VOCs, increasing the required ventilation rate.

2. Project Requirements

  • Regulations & Standards: Legal hygiene levels based on occupancy type.
  • Energy Efficiency: Specifying efficient fans or hybrid systems to minimize load.
  • Budget: Balancing capital cost vs lifetime operational cost.
  • Aesthetics & Noise: Hiding ductwork in suspended ceilings and ensuring systems are quiet in offices/bedrooms.

Day 4: Natural Ventilation

1. What drives Natural Ventilation?

Natural ventilation is caused by: Temperature differences (thermal density/buoyancy), Wind forces, or a combination of both.

2. Natural Strategies

  • Cross Ventilation: Wind-driven. Fresh air enters the high-pressure (windward) side, and stale air is drawn out the negative pressure side.
  • Stack Ventilation (Thermal Convection): Driven by buoyancy. Warmer, less dense air rises and escapes through high vents, creating negative pressure at the bottom that draws in cool air from low inlets.
  • Night Cooling (Night Flushing): Opening vents at night to remove daytime heat stored in the building fabric (floors/walls).
  • Single-sided Ventilation: Uses one opening. Common in narrow floor plans or noisy areas.

3. Pros & Cons

Merits: Zero energy cost, no mechanical noise, low maintenance, sustainable.

Demerits: Dependent on weather, variable airflow, cannot filter outdoor pollution, limited cooling capacity on hot/still days.

Day 5: Semi-Mechanical Systems

1. Mechanical Extract, Induced Inlet

Uses an extractor fan to pull air OUT, creating a negative pressure within the space. Air is naturally drawn IN from the outside or surrounding rooms.

  • Use Case: Lavatories, kitchens, and process areas.
  • Why? Odors and toxic gases will not escape to other areas because air is constantly flowing into the negative-pressure zone.

2. Mechanical Input, Forced Extract (Plenum System)

Air is forced INTO the space using a fan (often with a heater/filter), creating a positive pressure. Air naturally leaks OUT of the room.

  • Use Case: Hospital operating theatres.
  • Why? The positive pressure ensures that contaminated air from surrounding rooms cannot enter the sterile space. All leakages are strictly outwards.
  • Can supply untreated air, tempered air (heater battery), or warm air.

Day 6: Full Mechanical Systems

1. Mechanical Input & Mechanical Extract

Both a powered input fan and a powered extract fan are provided. The internal pressure depends on the ratio of extraction to input. If they handle the exact same amount of air, it creates a neutral condition.

2. Advantages of Full Mechanical Systems

  • Consistent Airflow: Not at the mercy of the weather or wind.
  • Controlled Direction: Precise routing of air.
  • Integration: Easily combined with Air Conditioning (HVAC) for temp/humidity control.
  • Filtration: Can aggressively filter out microorganisms, particulates, and outdoor pollution.

3. Disadvantages

  • High Costs: Increased energy consumption and routine maintenance costs.
  • Noise: Fans and ductwork generate noise/vibration.
  • Depressurization Risks: Overzealous extraction can cause back-drafting of dangerous appliance emissions (like carbon monoxide from furnaces) into the occupied space.
  • Complex Design: Requires careful architectural planning and massive ductwork space.
